# Blood tests and weight management: what can they tell you?

Blood tests can help investigate symptoms, assess particular health risks or monitor treatment. They cannot give every man a single explanation for why losing weight feels difficult. The appropriate tests depend on your health, medicines, history and the question being asked.

If you are losing weight without trying, speak to a GP rather than choosing a commercial panel to investigate it yourself. The [NHS advises getting continuing unintentional weight loss checked, especially when other symptoms are present](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/unintentional-weight-loss/). You do not need to wait until you have lost a particular percentage.

## Start with the question, then consider the test

“I’m tired all the time” and “I want to understand my cardiovascular risk” are different starting points. Tell a healthcare professional what has changed, how long it has been happening and how it affects ordinary life.

[NICE’s adult weight-management guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246/chapter/Identifying-and-assessing-overweight-obesity-and-central-adiposity) says to consider other conditions and relevant previous assessments. It also warns against attributing every symptom to weight. That does not establish one compulsory blood-test bundle for every adult trying to lose weight.

Take a list of medicines and supplements, previous results if you have them, and a short symptom timeline. Ask what each proposed investigation could change about your care.

## What are the common areas a clinician may consider?

The following explains test categories. It is not a shopping list everyone needs to buy.

### Cholesterol and other blood fats

A lipid profile can inform a discussion about cardiovascular risk. High cholesterol usually has no symptoms, according to the [NHS cholesterol-testing guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/high-cholesterol/getting-tested/). A result may be useful alongside other risk information; it does not explain your motivation or prove why your weight has changed.

### Blood sugar

Blood glucose and HbA1c provide different information. HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over roughly the previous two to three months, as described in [NHS type 1 diabetes information](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/type-1-diabetes/treatment/). It is not an instant picture of a rapidly changing problem. The appropriate test and interpretation belong with the clinician assessing you.

### Blood count and thyroid testing

The [NHS blood-test overview](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/blood-tests/) explains that a full blood count measures blood cells, while thyroid testing can help investigate thyroid conditions. Their usefulness depends on the clinical question. Tiredness alone is not enough to identify which result, if any, will explain it.

### Liver and kidney markers

Check the exact markers included and what the report can tell you. “Liver function” and “kidney function” are headings for particular investigations, rather than guarantees that every possible problem has been excluded.

Kidney assessment can require urine testing as well as blood tests, as the [NHS kidney-disease guidance explains](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/kidney-disease/diagnosis/). A blood-only package is not the complete assessment for every concern.

### Vitamins, minerals and hormones

Ask why these are being measured and what would happen with the result. A longer list of markers does not automatically make a package more appropriate for you. Do not start hormone treatment or supplements because of an isolated number without suitable advice.

## Do I need to fast?

Follow the instructions for your particular test. Most people having a cholesterol test do not need to fast, although some are asked to. The NHS cholesterol page explains this distinction.

Check preparation, timing and medicines with the responsible service. Do not stop prescribed medicine on the strength of a generic internet checklist.

## How can samples be collected?

A professional venous sample is taken from a vein. Some home tests use a finger-prick sample. SHIFT is also trialling an upper-arm collection device as another proposed home option.

The sample method must be suitable for the tests being measured. It does not change the need for an appropriate panel, laboratory process and explanation of the report.

[Explore SHIFT Bloods](/shift-health/blood-tests) to compare the current proposals. Baseline and Advanced are being developed with two home collection options. Complete currently requires professional venous collection. The upper-arm device, final marker lists, prices and reporting arrangements are being confirmed. Ordering is closed.

## What should happen after a result?

Keep the complete report and ask who explains it, who arranges any follow-up and when you should hear back. A result needs to be considered alongside the reason for testing. If symptoms persist, return to that concern even if the report looks reassuring.

Testing is one possible part of looking after your health. Food, activity, sleep, mental health, medicines and existing conditions still deserve attention. You can take practical steps and ask for help without treating a commercial blood panel as an entry requirement.
