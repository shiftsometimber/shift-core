Finger-prick kits put you off? Meet upper-arm blood collection

The idea of a home blood test can sound convenient right up to the bit where you have to prick your own finger. If that is where you lose interest, the collection method matters.

SHIFT is trialling another option: a small device that sits on your upper arm and collects a sample there. It gives us a different way to approach home collection for people who find finger-prick kits off-putting. The device, supported tests and final price are still being confirmed. Ordering is not open.

Why we’re exploring it

When you’re trying to understand your health, the practical details can decide whether you take the next step. Where do you go? What do you have to do yourself? Can you see what is involved before deciding?

We want those answers to be easy to find. That includes showing how collection works before asking anyone to choose a package. You should be able to compare the options, understand the limits and decide whether the process suits you.

For someone whose first reaction to a finger-prick kit is “absolutely not”, an upper-arm option may make that conversation easier. That is the reason we’re giving it proper space on SHIFT Bloods.

What is an upper-arm collection device?

Broadly, it is a device positioned on the upper arm to collect a small blood sample into a container. The exact mechanism, preparation and collection instructions depend on the model supplied.

Some devices in this category use a lancet to puncture the skin. An upper-arm device should not automatically be described as needle-free, painless or non-invasive. We won’t promise that you will feel nothing, or that everyone will find it easier.

A 2024 study published in PLOS ONE explored a particular self-collection device in Canadian research settings. It looked at sample volume, discomfort and acceptability. It supports taking this approach seriously, while also showing why collection systems need validation. It does not establish the performance of SHIFT’s proposed service or identify the device we will supply.

Does it change what the blood test checks?

The collection method and the laboratory panel are separate choices. The panel determines which markers are measured. The collection method determines how the sample is obtained.

A different collection device does not automatically add more markers, produce a diagnosis or make every panel suitable for home use. The laboratory needs a suitable sample for the exact tests requested.

Our current proposed options are:

  • Baseline Health: cholesterol, liver function and kidney function essentials, with finger-prick or upper-arm collection being trialled.
  • Advanced Health: the core areas plus thyroid, selected vitamins and minerals, and testosterone, with the two home methods being trialled.
  • Complete Health: a broader proposed panel requiring a professional venous sample, through a clinic or nurse home visit under the current proposal.

Final marker lists and supported collection methods will be confirmed before ordering opens. Compare the proposed SHIFT Bloods options.

Will the upper-arm option cost more?

A small extra charge is planned. The amount is being finalised, so there is no confirmed upgrade price to quote yet.

Before you pay for any blood-testing service, check the complete cost. Useful questions include whether the price covers return postage, laboratory analysis, explanation of results and any repeat collection if the sample cannot be analysed. Follow-up arrangements matter as much as the collection kit.

Can I see how it works first?

Yes. The SHIFT Bloods page includes an expandable section and a short illustrated overview. You can open it when you want more detail without scrolling through a long set of instructions.

The overview explains the proposed concept. It is not footage of the final supplied device or a substitute for its instructions. Once the exact model and laboratory process are confirmed, the service needs the matching demonstration and written guidance. We will not borrow instructions from a device that merely looks similar.

What if I’m worried about blood collection generally?

It is useful to say so. The NHS advises telling the professional taking your blood if needles worry you. If collecting a sample yourself feels unsuitable, ask a healthcare professional about an appropriate alternative.

If symptoms are the reason you are considering testing, start with the clinical question. A home kit should not delay assessment of a new or persistent health problem.

Our aim is straightforward: give you a clear view of what testing involves, including a collection option you may not have considered. You can then decide what to ask next, with the practical details in front of you.

SHIFT Bloods is coming soon. Ordering is closed. Explore the panels and collection overview.

Your blood-test results are back. What happens next?

A report full of numbers can leave you with more questions than you expected. What does the flag mean? Should you change something? Who is supposed to explain it?

The useful next step is to understand what was measured, what the report says and who can put it in context. Avoid making a treatment decision from a coloured flag or an isolated number.

Start with the reason for the test

Write one sentence describing why you had it. Perhaps a clinician was checking a symptom, monitoring a condition or reviewing a treatment. Perhaps you chose a private panel to learn more about particular markers.

Keep that question beside the report. “What does this mean for the problem I came with?” is more useful than trying to make every result look perfect.

If your original concern remains, ask what happens next even when the report looks reassuring. The NHS explains that further investigations can be needed depending on why a blood test was done.

Read the whole entry

For each result you want to discuss, keep the test name, value, units, laboratory reference range, sample date and any written comment together. A number copied without its units or report can be difficult to interpret.

A result outside the listed range needs context. Ask the responsible clinician what it means for you and whether any action is needed. Equally, a result within a range does not explain every symptom or assess parts of your health that were not tested.

Try these questions:

  • Is this result relevant to the reason I had the test?
  • Does it need action now, a repeat test or no further testing?
  • Could my medicines, supplements or the collection circumstances affect its interpretation?
  • If another test is needed, what question will it answer?

Avoid changing prescribed treatment or buying supplements to correct a flag before you have appropriate advice.

Different markers answer different questions

Cholesterol: a lipid result is part of a wider discussion about cardiovascular risk. The NHS describes considering heart attack and stroke risk alongside the result. It is not a score for your effort with food or exercise.

Kidney function: a blood result may be one part of the assessment. The NHS explains the role of both blood and urine tests. A package listing kidney markers does not mean it includes a urine test or every investigation a clinician might need.

A broader panel: more entries create more information to explain. They do not guarantee an explanation for tiredness, a weight plateau or every health concern. Check what each proposed panel actually contains rather than relying on its name.

Our existing guide to blood tests and weight management covers the wider clinical question. The SHIFT Bloods comparison shows the proposed packages and their current limits.

Make follow-up somebody’s named responsibility

Before buying a private test, ask who reviews the report, how you can ask a question and what happens if a result needs urgent attention. Check the hours and limits of the service, including who you should contact outside those hours.

“Results available online” does not answer those questions. A dashboard is a place to read information; you still need to know who is responsible for explaining and acting on it.

For SHIFT Bloods, reporting and clinical follow-up arrangements are still being confirmed with our specialist testing partner. Ordering remains closed. The My Timber example shows a proposed experience and does not establish a live monitoring or clinical review service.

Will a private result appear in my NHS record?

Do not assume so. NHS online access shows results added to or shared with your GP record. Buying a private panel does not by itself establish that sharing.

Ask the testing service how you receive a complete report and ask your GP practice how it accepts external results. Sharing a report also does not mean that a particular follow-up test or treatment has been agreed. Discuss the clinical question and next step directly.

Keep a copy you can find again, including the date and laboratory comments. If you have older reports, take those to the discussion too, rather than comparing numbers stripped of their context.

Leave the conversation with a practical plan

A useful plan says what happens, who is doing it and when you should hear back. For example: “I’ll contact the named reviewing service with the complete report and ask whether this needs a repeat test. I’ll confirm how and when they will respond.” Use the service’s actual arrangements.

If you feel unwell, seek appropriate medical advice based on your symptoms rather than waiting for a private report or an online reply. For a life-threatening emergency, call 999.

The report should help you have a clearer conversation about your health. You deserve an explanation you can understand and a next step you can actually follow.

Blood tests and weight management: what can they tell you?

Blood tests can help investigate symptoms, assess particular health risks or monitor treatment. They cannot give every man a single explanation for why losing weight feels difficult. The appropriate tests depend on your health, medicines, history and the question being asked.

If you are losing weight without trying, speak to a GP rather than choosing a commercial panel to investigate it yourself. The NHS advises getting continuing unintentional weight loss checked, especially when other symptoms are present. You do not need to wait until you have lost a particular percentage.

Start with the question, then consider the test

“I’m tired all the time” and “I want to understand my cardiovascular risk” are different starting points. Tell a healthcare professional what has changed, how long it has been happening and how it affects ordinary life.

NICE’s adult weight-management guidance says to consider other conditions and relevant previous assessments. It also warns against attributing every symptom to weight. That does not establish one compulsory blood-test bundle for every adult trying to lose weight.

Take a list of medicines and supplements, previous results if you have them, and a short symptom timeline. Ask what each proposed investigation could change about your care.

What are the common areas a clinician may consider?

The following explains test categories. It is not a shopping list everyone needs to buy.

Cholesterol and other blood fats

A lipid profile can inform a discussion about cardiovascular risk. High cholesterol usually has no symptoms, according to the NHS cholesterol-testing guidance. A result may be useful alongside other risk information; it does not explain your motivation or prove why your weight has changed.

Blood sugar

Blood glucose and HbA1c provide different information. HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over roughly the previous two to three months, as described in NHS type 1 diabetes information. It is not an instant picture of a rapidly changing problem. The appropriate test and interpretation belong with the clinician assessing you.

Blood count and thyroid testing

The NHS blood-test overview explains that a full blood count measures blood cells, while thyroid testing can help investigate thyroid conditions. Their usefulness depends on the clinical question. Tiredness alone is not enough to identify which result, if any, will explain it.

Liver and kidney markers

Check the exact markers included and what the report can tell you. “Liver function” and “kidney function” are headings for particular investigations, rather than guarantees that every possible problem has been excluded.

Kidney assessment can require urine testing as well as blood tests, as the NHS kidney-disease guidance explains. A blood-only package is not the complete assessment for every concern.

Vitamins, minerals and hormones

Ask why these are being measured and what would happen with the result. A longer list of markers does not automatically make a package more appropriate for you. Do not start hormone treatment or supplements because of an isolated number without suitable advice.

Do I need to fast?

Follow the instructions for your particular test. Most people having a cholesterol test do not need to fast, although some are asked to. The NHS cholesterol page explains this distinction.

Check preparation, timing and medicines with the responsible service. Do not stop prescribed medicine on the strength of a generic internet checklist.

How can samples be collected?

A professional venous sample is taken from a vein. Some home tests use a finger-prick sample. SHIFT is also trialling an upper-arm collection device as another proposed home option.

The sample method must be suitable for the tests being measured. It does not change the need for an appropriate panel, laboratory process and explanation of the report.

Explore SHIFT Bloods to compare the current proposals. Baseline and Advanced are being developed with two home collection options. Complete currently requires professional venous collection. The upper-arm device, final marker lists, prices and reporting arrangements are being confirmed. Ordering is closed.

What should happen after a result?

Keep the complete report and ask who explains it, who arranges any follow-up and when you should hear back. A result needs to be considered alongside the reason for testing. If symptoms persist, return to that concern even if the report looks reassuring.

Testing is one possible part of looking after your health. Food, activity, sleep, mental health, medicines and existing conditions still deserve attention. You can take practical steps and ask for help without treating a commercial blood panel as an entry requirement.
