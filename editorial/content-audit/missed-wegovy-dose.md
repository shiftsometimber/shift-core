# Missed a Wegovy injection: the UK leaflet’s rules

You remembered the shopping, the school run and that work thing you were meant to finish yesterday. The injection slipped through. Now the calendar is open and every website seems to have a different answer.

Start with the exact medicine in your hand. This page is about **UK Wegovy weekly injections**, not Wegovy tablets, Ozempic or Mounjaro. Their instructions are not interchangeable. If your own leaflet or prescriber has given you different instructions, ask your pharmacist or prescriber to resolve that difference.

**Checked against UK product information on 2 October 2026. This is general information, not an individual restart or prescribing decision.**

## The single missed-dose rule

The UK [Wegovy patient leaflet](https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/files/pil.13800.pdf) distinguishes two situations:

- **Five days or less have passed since the injection was due:** use the missed dose as soon as you remember, then take the next dose on your usual scheduled day.
- **More than five days have passed:** skip the missed injection and take the next dose on your usual scheduled day.
- **Do not double the dose** to make up for a missed one.

The boundary matters: the leaflet includes day five in the first case. An article saying “five days or more means skip” changes that instruction.

Count from when the injection was due, not from when you noticed you had forgotten it. If you cannot work out the dates, do not fill the gap with a best guess. Your dispensing pharmacist or prescriber can help you check what the instructions mean for your situation.

## A missed dose does not automatically create a new weekly day

The missed-dose instructions say to return to the **usual scheduled day**. They do not tell everyone to put the next injection seven days after the late one.

Deliberately changing your regular injection day is a separate instruction in the [UK Summary of Product Characteristics](https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/13800/smpc). It specifies a gap of more than 72 hours between doses when changing the day. That is not a reason to invent a different catch-up schedule.

If the missed-dose instructions and a planned day change leave you uncertain, check with your pharmacist or prescriber before acting. This page cannot choose a dosing date for you.

## More than one dose missed, or a longer break

A longer interruption needs a conversation with the service responsible for your prescription. The product information says a lower starting dose may need to be considered when restarting after more doses have been missed. It does not provide one restart strength that is right for everybody.

Do not choose a lower or higher strength from an online table, use somebody else's pen, or try to make up all the missing injections. Your prescriber needs to know the gap, what you were taking and how you were tolerating it.

You can make that conversation easier without needing to become a medication expert. Write down:

1. The exact medicine, pen strength and dispensing pharmacy.
2. The date of your last injection, if known.
3. The date the next injection was due and which doses you missed.
4. Any symptoms you have now or had before the gap.
5. Other medicines and relevant changes in your health.

If a date is approximate, say so. Accurate uncertainty is more useful than a confident invented date.

## Symptoms change the priority

A calendar problem and a concerning symptom are different jobs. Severe, persistent stomach pain, especially if it spreads to your back or comes with sickness, needs urgent medical attention. The [MHRA's pancreatitis warning](https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/glp-1-receptor-agonists-and-dual-glp-1-slash-gip-receptor-agonists-strengthened-warnings-on-acute-pancreatitis-including-necrotising-and-fatal-cases) applies to these medicines.

Repeated vomiting, inability to keep fluids down or signs of serious dehydration also need medical advice. Use appropriate urgent NHS care when needed; do not wait for a routine company inbox.

Tell the clinician which medicine you use, including privately prescribed treatment. If pancreatitis is confirmed, the MHRA advises that treatment must not be restarted. A clinician should assess the symptoms and treatment; a website cannot diagnose what is happening.

## Make the next week less dependent on memory

Once the medicine question is settled, choose a reminder system you will actually notice. A weekly phone reminder and a note of the date you really took the injection are more useful than three different apps with three different dates.

Keep the record simple:

| Record | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Regular day | The day agreed for your normal schedule |
| Actual injection | Date and medicine/strength used |
| Missed or delayed | What happened, without filling in an injection you did not take |
| Advice received | Who advised you, when, and the instructions they gave |

When travelling or changing routines, take the leaflet and pharmacy contact details with you. Discuss uncertainties rather than borrowing a rule for a different product or country.

You do not need a perfect streak. You need a record that tells the truth when you look back at it.
