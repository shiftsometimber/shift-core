TESTOSTERONE & ENERGY · INVESTIGATE, DON’T ASSUME

Not feeling yourself?
Start with the right questions.

Less energy. Less interest in sex. Strength or mood that feels different. If something has changed and it is sticking around, you deserve to understand why.

Testosterone could be part of the picture. Start with what feels familiar, then find the next conversation worth having.

START WITH YOUR REASON

What’s brought you here?

Open whichever sounds like you. These prompts help you find your way through the guide; they do not diagnose low testosterone.

I’m shattered—even after a night’s sleep.

Start with the pattern: when does your energy dip, how long has it been happening, and does sleep feel refreshing? Mention snoring or broken sleep in your GP conversation. Tiredness alone cannot tell you whether testosterone is involved.

My sex drive just isn’t what it was.

Is it less interest in sex, difficulty getting or keeping an erection, or both? Those are different concerns and can overlap. You do not need to work out the cause before asking for help.

My strength or motivation has dropped.

What feels harder than before—everyday tasks, exercise, or getting started at all? Note when it changed and anything else happening with sleep, mood, work or medicines. That context is more useful than guessing at a hormone level.

I’ve had a testosterone result. Now what?

Low, borderline or within range: a number needs context. Keep the report, its laboratory range and the collection time for the clinician who requested it. Ask whether repeat testing or another explanation needs consideration.

01 · UNDERSTAND WHAT HAS CHANGED

A proper assessment starts with you.

Persistent tiredness, lower sex drive or a change in mood can have several explanations. Sleep, stress, medicines and other health conditions may be involved. Getting older is not, by itself, a reason to start testosterone. NHS guidance →

The conversation

A clinician asks about symptoms, medical history, medicines and supplements, and examines you where needed. Tell them about fertility plans and any previous hormone or anabolic steroid use.

The blood tests

Testing usually involves morning blood samples, often fasting, with a low result repeated on another day. Follow the clinician’s instructions; mention shift work, recent illness and disrupted sleep.

The interpretation

Results need clinical context. Further hormone tests may help establish the cause. A low or borderline number alone does not settle whether you need treatment.

Assessment background: Society for Endocrinology — male hypogonadism.

What if the result is normal?

Your symptoms still matter. Ask your GP what else could explain them and when to review things if they continue. A result within the laboratory range is a reason to interpret the wider picture, not dismiss how you feel.

02 · UNDERSTAND THE TREATMENT

TRT has a job. It is not a general boost.

Testosterone replacement therapy replaces testosterone in men with a confirmed deficiency when a clinician judges it appropriate. A diagnosis normally combines relevant symptoms with consistently low results. Clinical guideline →

POTENTIAL BENEFITS

Help with a confirmed deficiency.

For suitable patients, treatment may improve sexual symptoms and support bone and muscle health. Benefits vary; it cannot promise more confidence, better relationships or an overnight return to your old self.

THE TRADE-OFFS

Real treatment. Real follow-up.

Possible problems include acne, raised red blood cell levels and reduced sperm production. Some medical histories make TRT unsuitable or require specialist advice. It is not a bodybuilding, performance-enhancement or anti-ageing service.

Benefits and risks: Endocrine Society — patient information.

03 · HAVE THIS CONVERSATION FIRST

Want children?
Say so before treatment.

TRT can suppress sperm production. Tell the clinician if you are trying for a baby, might want children later, or are unsure. Testosterone is not a fertility treatment.

Guidelines advise against starting TRT in men planning fertility in the near term. Ask about specialist assessment and options suited to your circumstances before starting anything. Read the guideline →

Your next move: put your fertility plans on the list for your appointment.

04 · CARE DOES NOT END AT THE PRESCRIPTION

Know who is following you up.

If TRT is prescribed, agree a monitoring plan: symptom response, side effects, testosterone and red blood cell checks, plus prostate assessment where appropriate. The clinician sets the timing and decides whether treatment should continue or change. Monitoring background →

Ask before you start

  • Who reviews my results, and how do I reach them?
  • Which tests and appointments are included in the cost?
  • What happens if treatment is not helping or I get side effects?
  • Who supports me if I need to stop or the provider changes?

Already taking testosterone? Discuss changes or stopping with your prescriber. Your prescriber can explain a plan suited to your circumstances.

MAKE THE CONVERSATION EASIER

Before you speak to your GP.

You do not need the right medical words. A few notes on paper or your phone can help you explain what has changed.

Bring your own context

  • What has changed, and roughly when?
  • How is it affecting your everyday life?
  • What has changed with sleep, work, medicines or supplements?
  • Could having children matter to you now or later?

Three questions worth asking

  1. Could something else explain how I’m feeling?
  2. Would testing help—and what happens after the result?
  3. If treatment is suggested, who handles my follow-up?

Your next move: take those notes to your appointment. You are asking for help understanding the change, not trying to prove a diagnosis.

DO NOT WAIT FOR A HORMONE TEST

Some symptoms need help now.

  • Sudden severe testicular pain: go to A&E immediately or call 999. NHS advice.
  • A new testicular lump or swelling: arrange a GP appointment; do not self-diagnose or wait for this service. NHS advice.
  • A sudden, extremely painful headache, or headache with loss of vision: call 999 or go to A&E. For headache with other new vision problems, seek urgent GP or NHS 111 advice. NHS advice.
  • You cannot keep yourself or someone else safe: call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent mental-health help without immediate danger, call 111 and select the mental-health option in England, or use your local urgent service. NHS advice.

Unsure where to turn? NHS 111 online can help with urgent medical advice in England.
