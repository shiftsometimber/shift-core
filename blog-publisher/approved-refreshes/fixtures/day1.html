Understanding obesity in men

The World Health Organization describes obesity as a chronic disease influenced by biological, environmental, social and behavioural factors. That description helps explain why support may need to continue over time. It does not tell us what is happening in one particular person, or which approach is right for them. [1]

You do not have to adopt a label before asking for help. Your health, the things you want to do and the choices you are comfortable with matter.

“Male obesity” means obesity in men. It is not a separate diagnosis with one cause or one solution. This page focuses on situations men may recognise, without assuming that every bloke has the same life or wants the same outcome.

The NHS describes obesity as having excess body fat. Body mass index (BMI) is one way healthcare professionals assess weight, but it cannot distinguish muscle from fat and does not describe your whole health. A healthcare conversation can put measurements, symptoms and your circumstances together. This page cannot diagnose you. [2]

Check your BMI — one useful number, not the whole picture. BMI is a screening measure. It does not measure body fat or decide which support is right for you. Use the result as a starting point for questions, then choose a practical next step.

You may want to feel more comfortable, look after your health, or keep up with things you enjoy. You may be uncertain about losing weight at all. You can ask questions and consider support without committing to a purchase or a particular treatment.

What do my weight and waist measurements mean?

Male obesity means obesity in men: excess body fat that can affect health. BMI and waist measurements help assess risk; neither tells the whole story or diagnoses every health problem. Your symptoms, muscle mass, family background and circumstances matter too. [5]

What BMI is classed as obesity for a man?

The usual adult categories are the same for men and women. BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It cannot distinguish fat from muscle, so a muscular man’s result needs careful interpretation. [5]

Standard adult BMI categories (kg/m²)
BMICategory
Below 18.5Underweight
18.5 to below 25Healthy weight category
25 to below 30Overweight
30 to below 35Obesity, class 1
35 to below 40Obesity, class 2
40 or aboveObesity, class 3

Family background changes the interpretation. NICE recommends lower thresholds for people with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African–Caribbean background: overweight from BMI 23 to below 27.5, and obesity from 27.5. Class 2 and 3 thresholds are usually reduced by 2.5. These reflect health risk occurring at lower BMI; they are not different standards of effort. [5]

A result in the healthy-weight category does not rule out excess fat around the middle. BMI also needs caution in people aged 65 and over. A calculator result is a starting point for a conversation, not automatic eligibility for treatment. [5]

What waist size should a man pay attention to?

Fat around the abdomen can add health risk that BMI alone misses. For adults with BMI below 35, NICE recommends using waist-to-height ratio alongside BMI. [5]

Waist-to-height ratio: adults with BMI below 35
RatioInterpretation
0.4 to below 0.5Healthy central-adiposity category
0.5 to below 0.6Increased central adiposity and associated health risk
0.6 or aboveHigh central adiposity and further increased risk

The practical message is to keep your waist below half your height. At 180cm tall (about 5ft 11in), a 90cm waist (about 35.4in) gives a ratio of 0.5. This is a screening signal, not a diagnosis. Ratios below 0.4 are not a target to chase. [5]

You may also see 94cm (about 37in) and 102cm (about 40in) quoted for men. These are commonly used waist reference points for White European men: above 94cm indicates increased risk, and above 102cm greater risk. They are not universal cut-offs for every ethnic background. Use the height-based guidance and your wider health picture rather than treating a single centimetre as a verdict. [7]

How do I measure my waist?

  1. Find the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hips.
  2. Place the tape halfway between them, around your body.
  3. Breathe out naturally and take the measurement without pulling the tape tight.

Use the same units for height and waist. Measure your body, not the label in your jeans. [5]

Why does obesity matter for men’s health?

Some concerns are specific to male reproductive health; others affect men and women. None of the associations below proves you have a condition, or that weight explains a symptom. Pick the concern that matters to you and ask the useful question. You do not need to wait until you have lost weight.

Blood sugar, heart health and blood pressure

Obesity is associated with higher risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease and high blood pressure. [2] A sensible question is: “Given my measurements and family history, should we check my blood pressure, blood sugar or cholesterol?”

Understand prediabetes and your next questions · Look at heart health beyond weight. These explain the wider picture; reading them does not arrange a test.

Snoring, breathing pauses and exhaustion

Obesity is linked to obstructive sleep apnoea. Breathing pauses, gasping during sleep or persistent daytime tiredness deserve a GP conversation; weight loss alone is not a substitute for assessment. [8] Ask: “Could my sleep symptoms need investigation?” Read about sleep apnoea and weight.

Testosterone, sex drive and erections

Men living with obesity can have lower measured testosterone, but a low result or tiredness alone does not establish testosterone deficiency. Symptoms and correctly interpreted blood tests matter. [9] “Could something else explain this change in energy or sex drive?” is a useful starting question. Understand the weight–testosterone connection.

Repeated erection difficulties can overlap with diabetes, blood pressure, medicines, stress or hormone problems. The NHS advises seeing a GP or sexual-health clinic if they keep happening. [10] Tell them what has changed and when. Do not assume it is simply your weight, or that losing weight will definitely resolve it.

Fertility and plans for a family

NICE says men with BMI 30 or above have an increased risk of reduced fertility. That does not mean you cannot conceive, or that weight loss guarantees a pregnancy. [11] If having children matters to you, bring that into the healthcare conversation now: “Could my health or current treatment affect fertility, and what should we investigate?”

Fatty liver: why feeling fine does not settle it

Fatty liver disease, also called MASLD, is associated with excess weight, particularly around the waist. It often has no symptoms. [12] A waist measurement cannot diagnose it. If you are worried, ask your GP whether your risk factors or existing results warrant further assessment.

Joints, movement and everyday comfort

Obesity is associated with osteoarthritis, but knee pain has other possible causes too. [2] Ask what is causing the pain and what movement is suitable for you, rather than trying to push through it. Read about knee pain and weight.

Mood deserves attention alongside physical health

If shame, low mood or eating difficulties are affecting everyday life, they deserve help in their own right. You do not have to reach a particular weight first. Read our weight and mood guide, and use the eating-difficulty signposting below. [2]

Your next useful move: choose one concern, note what changed and how it affects your day, then take that to your GP or existing treating service. You are asking for understanding, not trying to prove a diagnosis.

Why can I be trying hard and still struggle?

Weight management involves more than a decision to try harder. Food availability, money, caring responsibilities, work patterns and opportunities to move can shape what is possible. Health conditions and some medicines can also affect weight. [1], [2]

Energy balance still matters. The practical challenge is finding an adequate eating pattern and suitable activity that you can sustain, with further help where needed. A plan that only works on your easiest day needs adapting.

What does “biological resistance” mean?

A primary study found that increased hunger and changes in appetite-related hormones could persist a year after diet-induced weight loss. It involved 50 adults without diabetes starting a very-low-energy diet; it does not predict what will happen to every man. [3]

This explains a possible part of the picture. It is not a diagnosis that your body is broken, or proof that ordinary changes cannot help. If you are concerned, describe the pattern to a healthcare professional rather than assuming one explanation fits everything.

Why do I keep putting weight back on?

Weight regain can reflect biology, circumstances and changes in the support or routine that was helping. Maintaining progress often needs its own plan. It is worth noticing what has become harder and asking for help, rather than responding with increasingly harsh food rules. [3], [5]

If you weigh yourself, compare a longer pattern under similar conditions. One reading cannot explain what is happening. You can also notice whether an everyday task feels easier. Tracking is optional; if it increases distress, discuss another way to review progress.

What if I feel embarrassed asking?

You can start with one sentence: “I have tried to manage my weight several times, and I would like help finding an approach I can keep using.” You do not need a perfect food diary or a polished explanation.

If mood, shame or eating difficulties are affecting daily life, ask for help with those too. Weight loss is not a substitute for mental-health care. If you are experiencing loss of control around eating, compensating for food or distress about tracking, a more restrictive plan may be the wrong next step.

Read about weight, mood and asking for support.

Making changes around work and family

A plan has to survive your real week. Long shifts, caring for someone, driving between jobs, limited money or getting the kids fed can make the tidy version on paper impossible. That is useful information about the plan.

Look at the point where things usually unravel. Do you arrive home hungry with nothing sorted? Is a break unpredictable? Does a routine disappear when someone needs you? Pick that moment rather than trying to change everything at once.

A workable request to someone at home might be: “I’m trying to sort lunch for the busy days. Can we agree one option we can keep in?” If you want support, say what would help: company, a practical reminder, or fewer comments about your weight. You decide what you share.

Getting appropriate help

No single approach suits everyone. Support can include everyday changes, structured programmes, psychological help, medication or surgery where appropriate. A GP can discuss your health and appropriate services. The decision should take your needs and preferences into account. [2], [4]

If you already receive treatment elsewhere, your treating service remains responsible for clinical decisions. You can still use general practical support from SHIFT. Ask that service about symptoms, monitoring or changes to treatment.

A useful starting question for your GP is: “What support is available locally, and how do I access it?” Availability and referral routes differ across the UK. If you are already receiving care, ask that service how the practical changes you want to make fit your plan. SHIFT does not arrange a referral or decide clinical suitability through this page.

If eating feels out of control or causes significant distress, ask a GP for help. Binge eating is a condition for which support is available; this page cannot tell you whether you have it. Do not respond by making your food rules increasingly restrictive. Read the NHS explanation of binge eating.

Maintaining progress needs its own plan

Keeping progress is a reason to keep getting support. Think about the routines that helped and which ones you still want. Maintenance need not mean repeating the most demanding version of a weight-loss plan indefinitely. It means reviewing what is suitable for your circumstances. [5]

Before a busy week, choose a routine, a likely disruption and a fallback. For example: “I’ll organise lunch before my early shift. If I run out of time, I’ll use the option I’ve already chosen.” Keep the fallback affordable and available. It should solve the practical problem, not punish you for a change of plan.

A maintenance example: “I want to keep having lunch during my early shift. On Sunday I’ll check what is in the cupboard and choose Monday’s lunch. If the shift changes, I’ll take a sandwich instead.” After the week, ask: did I get a lunch break, did the food keep me going, and was it affordable and easy enough? If preparation was too much effort, choose one ready-to-assemble option. If lunch was sorted but evening hunger was still the problem, review that part of the day instead or ask for help.

If your weight changes, look at the pattern and what else has changed. If you are worried about your health, appetite or the effects of treatment, ask the appropriate healthcare professional. SHIFT can help organise everyday routines alongside care; we do not tell you to start, stop or alter treatment.

Read about a weight-loss plateau if you are unsure whether your longer pattern has stalled. Make a plan for keeping useful routines if maintaining progress is your priority.

ONE THING YOU CAN SORT TODAY

Find a step that fits your day

Choose one of these if it is useful. Reading and thinking it over is a valid start too. These are organising ideas, not a prescribed diet or exercise plan.

I get home hungry and have no plan

Choose tomorrow’s lunch or evening meal now. Check that you have what you need and name a simpler fallback for a late finish. No overhaul required.

A worked example: “Tomorrow I’ll take a wholemeal sandwich with chicken, beans or hummus, plus whatever fruit or veg I have. If I finish late or forget it, I’ll use beans on toast and frozen veg at home.” Choose food you enjoy that suits your dietary needs. These are meal ideas, not a calorie target or a promise of weight loss.

No fridge at work? Choose ingredients you can store safely, use a cool bag where needed, or buy a suitable option nearby. No microwave? Choose a cold meal. Tight budget? Use what you already have. If cooking is too much today, make the fallback a meal you can assemble.
