Your blood-test results are back. What happens next?

Published 19 October 2026 · SHIFT Team

A report full of numbers can leave you with more questions than you expected. What does the flag mean? Should you change something? Who is supposed to explain it?

The useful next step is to understand what was measured, what the report says and who can put it in context. Avoid making a treatment decision from a coloured flag or an isolated number.

Start with the reason for the test

Write one sentence describing why you had it. Perhaps a clinician was checking a symptom, monitoring a condition or reviewing a treatment. Perhaps you chose a private panel to learn more about particular markers.

Keep that question beside the report. “What does this mean for the problem I came with?” is more useful than trying to make every result look perfect.

If your original concern remains, ask what happens next even when the report looks reassuring. The NHS explains that further investigations can be needed depending on why a blood test was done.

Read the whole entry

For each result you want to discuss, keep the test name, value, units, laboratory reference range, sample date and any written comment together. A number copied without its units or report can be difficult to interpret.

A result outside the listed range needs context. Ask the responsible clinician what it means for you and whether any action is needed. Equally, a result within a range does not explain every symptom or assess parts of your health that were not tested.

Try these questions:

  • Is this result relevant to the reason I had the test?
  • Does it need action now, a repeat test or no further testing?
  • Could my medicines, supplements or the collection circumstances affect its interpretation?
  • If another test is needed, what question will it answer?

Avoid changing prescribed treatment or buying supplements to correct a flag before you have appropriate advice.

Different markers answer different questions

Cholesterol: a lipid result is part of a wider discussion about cardiovascular risk. The NHS describes considering heart attack and stroke risk alongside the result. It is not a score for your effort with food or exercise.

Kidney function: a blood result may be one part of the assessment. The NHS explains the role of both blood and urine tests. A package listing kidney markers does not mean it includes a urine test or every investigation a clinician might need.

A broader panel: more entries create more information to explain. They do not guarantee an explanation for tiredness, a weight plateau or every health concern. Check what each proposed panel actually contains rather than relying on its name.

Our existing guide to blood tests and weight management covers the wider clinical question. The SHIFT Bloods comparison shows the proposed packages and their current limits.

Make follow-up somebody’s named responsibility

Before buying a private test, ask who reviews the report, how you can ask a question and what happens if a result needs urgent attention. Check the hours and limits of the service, including who you should contact outside those hours.

“Results available online” does not answer those questions. A dashboard is a place to read information; you still need to know who is responsible for explaining and acting on it.

Will a private result appear in my NHS record?

Do not assume so. NHS online access shows results added to or shared with your GP record. Buying a private panel does not by itself establish that sharing.

Ask the testing service how you receive a complete report and ask your GP practice how it accepts external results. Sharing a report also does not mean that a particular follow-up test or treatment has been agreed. Discuss the clinical question and next step directly.

Keep a copy you can find again, including the date and laboratory comments. If you have older reports, take those to the discussion too, rather than comparing numbers stripped of their context.

Leave the conversation with a practical plan

A useful plan says what happens, who is doing it and when you should hear back. For example: “I’ll contact the named reviewing service with the complete report and ask whether this needs a repeat test. I’ll confirm how and when they will respond.” Use the service’s actual arrangements.

If you feel unwell, seek appropriate medical advice based on your symptoms rather than waiting for a private report or an online reply. For a life-threatening emergency, call 999.

The report should help you have a clearer conversation about your health. You deserve an explanation you can understand and a next step you can actually follow.

General information for UK adult readers; NHS online-record guidance cited here relates to the NHS England service. Sources checked 18 October 2026. Independent clinical review is not claimed.
