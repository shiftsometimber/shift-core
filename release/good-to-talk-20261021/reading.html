You don't have to know what to call it

Mental-health problems do not always arrive with a neat label. NHS guidance lists feelings such as stress, anxiety, low mood, loneliness, grief and anger among common experiences people may need help with. If the way you're feeling is affecting everyday life, relationships, work, sleep or your ability to cope, that is reason enough to take it seriously.

Which kind of help do I need?

You do not need a diagnosis before asking for help. Start with the problem you want somebody to understand: “I’m not sleeping”, “I’m snapping at people”, “I feel flat”, or “I’m avoiding everything”. The NHS has information on these feelings; a webpage cannot tell you what is causing them.

Choose the job you need help with

  • Understanding what is happening: read the guide closest to your experience. Write down what changed and what it is affecting.
  • Having someone listen: use a trusted person or an appropriate listening service. You do not need to arrive with an explanation or a finished plan.
  • Getting an assessment or treatment: contact your GP or the relevant NHS mental-health service. In England, some NHS talking-therapy services accept self-referrals. Check the local service’s criteria and contact route.
  • Help that cannot safely wait: go directly to the urgent-help information above or the UK urgent-support guide.

Make the first conversation easier

If your mind goes blank when somebody asks “What’s wrong?”, take three notes: what changed; what daily life has become harder; what you want help with. For example: “For the last few weeks I’ve been waking early, avoiding friends and struggling to concentrate at work. I’d like help understanding it and deciding what to do.” Use your own details; this is an illustration, not a diagnostic checklist.

Ask what happens next, how to contact the service while waiting and what to do if things worsen. If making contact is the obstacle, ask someone you trust to sit with you or help prepare the information. Tell the service about communication or access needs.

What does “free” mean here?

Good to Talk’s public guides are free to read without a SHIFT membership or medicine purchase. That does not mean every external service is free, immediately available or suitable for everyone. Check any provider’s costs, eligibility and waiting arrangements before agreeing to something.

Use our guide to supporting someone who refuses help if you are here for another person. For your own next conversation, read NHS guidance on accessing services.
