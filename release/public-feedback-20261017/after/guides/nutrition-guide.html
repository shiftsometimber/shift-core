The Practical Men's Nutrition Guide

Eating well starts with meals you can afford, prepare and repeat. Use the overall pattern of your week to improve variety and balance, then make one change to the shopping or cooking that keeps getting in the way. You do not need a perfect menu or an expensive supplement routine.

Balance the week, not every mouthful

The NHS Eatwell Guide describes an overall eating pattern. Fruit and vegetables, higher-fibre starchy foods, protein foods and dairy or suitable alternatives each have a place. Balance can be built across the day or week. A meal that is less varied can be followed by another that supplies what was missing; it does not require punishment or starting again.

Build three reliable meal options

Choose a breakfast, a packed lunch and an evening meal you genuinely like. For example, try porridge with fruit; a wholemeal sandwich with a filling and salad; or potatoes, beans or chicken and vegetables. These are adaptable meal ideas, not prescribed portions. Check ingredients against your allergies, dietary needs and any professional advice.

Use carbohydrates deliberately

Bread, rice, pasta and potatoes can be part of a balanced diet. Higher-fibre versions are useful options where you enjoy them. Look at the complete meal, including butter, oil, sauces and drinks, rather than treating one food as the entire problem. A portion that suits one person may not suit another.

Make the shopping list do some work

  • Choose the meals before adding extras to the basket.
  • Use frozen vegetables and suitable tinned foods when they make preparation and cost easier.
  • Keep one quick option for a late finish, with ingredients you can safely store.
  • Check the total price, usable portions and food you are likely to waste rather than buying a health claim on the label.

Handle takeaways and busy days

Decide what you actually want before ordering. Compare the full meal: main, sides, sauce, drink and likely leftovers. If a large portion is usual, sharing or saving some safely may be a practical option. Make tomorrow's ordinary meal easier rather than compensating through fasting or a punishing workout.

When you need individual nutrition advice

General meal ideas do not cover every situation. Allergies, kidney disease, an eating disorder, diabetes medicines, surgery or difficulty eating enough can change what is suitable. Tell the appropriate clinician or dietitian what is happening. If a weight-loss medicine makes regular eating difficult, ask the prescribing team for advice instead of assuming less food is always better.

Review the obstacle after a week

Ask which meal kept failing and why: time, cost, appetite, taste or preparation. Change that part of the plan. Keep the options that worked. Shift Grub in My Timber can help you save choices, but a recipe collection cannot confirm that a menu meets your individual clinical needs.
