The 30-second answer

A gastric balloon is a temporary device placed inside the stomach to take up space, increase fullness and help reduce food intake. Most traditional balloons are placed endoscopically and removed after around 6–12 months; swallowable systems are designed to pass naturally after a shorter period.

It is less invasive than bariatric surgery, but the trade-off is usually less weight loss and a stronger reliance on maintaining habits after the balloon is gone.

What is a gastric balloon?

A gastric balloon is a soft balloon that sits in the stomach. Traditional systems are inserted through the mouth using an endoscope and then filled with saline or air. South Tees and Hull NHS information describe it as a short-term tool that reduces hunger and helps people feel full sooner.

What kinds of balloon exist?

Endoscopic fluid-filled balloon

Inserted under endoscopy and removed later by endoscopy. Common examples stay 6 or 12 months depending on device.

Swallowable balloon

Swallowed as a capsule, filled after imaging confirmation, then designed to empty and pass naturally after several months.

Bridge-to-surgery balloon

Sometimes used to reduce weight and operative risk before another procedure.

How does the balloon work?

The balloon occupies space and can slow gastric emptying, helping you feel full sooner. It is not a hormonal medicine and it does not permanently change your anatomy.

How much weight could I lose?

Results vary by balloon system, treatment length and behavioural support. Chelsea and Westminster NHS says average loss can be up to around 20 kg through balloon treatment combined with diet and activity. Somerset NHS reported average losses around 10–15% of body weight with the Allurion programme after approximately four months.

Those figures are from different devices and patient groups, so they should not be treated as interchangeable promises.

Who might a balloon suit?

  • Someone wanting a temporary, non-surgical intervention.
  • Someone needing moderate rather than very large weight loss.
  • Someone who cannot or does not want bariatric surgery.
  • Someone using it as a bridge to a safer later operation.
  • Someone willing to use the balloon period to build habits for after removal.

Is gastric balloon available on the NHS?

Some NHS bariatric services use intragastric balloons, often for selected patients or as a bridge to surgery rather than as a routine long-term obesity treatment for everyone. Availability varies by region and centre.

Private gastric balloon treatment

Private balloon programmes should include more than insertion. Ask what dietetic support, anti-sickness medication, emergency contact, removal, follow-up and behavioural support are included.

What is insertion like?

Traditional balloons are usually passed through the mouth using an endoscope. Depending on the device and service, sedation or anaesthesia may be used. The balloon is then filled inside the stomach.

Swallowable balloons follow a different pathway and may not require endoscopy for placement or removal.

Why can the first few days be rough?

Nausea, cramping and vomiting are common as the stomach adjusts to a foreign object. Services often prescribe anti-sickness and acid-suppression medication and use a liquid diet initially.

Persistent inability to keep fluids down needs medical review.

Dehydration can become serious quickly.

Eating with a balloon

Diet progresses from fluids to soft food and then small normal meals. The balloon works best when people avoid grazing and calorie-dense liquids and use the provider’s support programme to learn slower eating, portion awareness and better food choices.

How long does it stay in?

Chelsea and Westminster NHS says balloons can remain in place around 6–12 months depending on the device. Leaving a balloon longer than intended can increase the risk of material deterioration and deflation.

How is the balloon removed?

Traditional systems are emptied and removed endoscopically. Some swallowable systems are designed to empty through a valve and pass naturally through the digestive tract after several months.

What can go wrong?

Common

  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Cramping
  • Reflux
  • Abdominal discomfort

Less common but important

  • Dehydration
  • Ulceration
  • Balloon deflation
  • Migration / obstruction
  • Need for early removal

What happens if a balloon deflates or migrates?

A deflated balloon can move out of the stomach. Some devices use coloured fluid so urine colour change can warn of leakage. Migration can rarely lead to bowel obstruction and urgent treatment.

Balloon vs weight-loss medication

A balloon is temporary and mechanical; medication is systemic and often longer-term. Medication may produce greater or more sustained weight loss for some people, but requires ongoing prescribing and may have recurring cost. Balloon treatment avoids long-term drug exposure but ends when the device leaves.

Balloon vs surgery

A balloon does not cut or permanently alter anatomy and usually has faster recovery. Sleeve and bypass generally produce larger and more durable average weight loss but carry surgical and lifelong nutritional consequences.

Balloon vs ESG

ESG uses endoscopic sutures to reduce stomach volume and is intended as a more durable anatomical change than a temporary balloon. It is still less invasive than conventional surgery.

What happens after the balloon is gone?

This is the real test. Hunger and stomach capacity are no longer mechanically affected by the device, so weight can return if no sustainable habits or follow-up plan exist. The balloon should be treated as a temporary training window, not six months of rented willpower.

Questions to ask a balloon provider

  • Which balloon system do you use?
  • How long does it stay?
  • Is removal included?
  • What happens if I cannot tolerate it?
  • Who manages severe vomiting?
  • What weight loss do you see in your own patients?
  • What behavioural support is included?
  • What happens after removal?

Shift Says

Our take

The balloon can be a useful middle ground: more than lifestyle-only, less permanent than surgery. But temporary treatment needs a permanent plan.

If follow-up ends the day the balloon comes out, we'd be wary.

Printable balloon checklist

  • I know which balloon I am getting.
  • I know how it is inserted and removed.
  • I understand first-week nausea/vomiting risk.
  • I know what support is included.
  • I have a plan for after removal.
  • I have compared balloon with medication and ESG.

FAQs

How long does a gastric balloon stay in?

Typically around 6–12 months for traditional systems, depending on device; swallowable balloons may pass after a shorter period.

Is a balloon surgery?

No. It is an intragastric device and does not involve cutting the stomach.

How much weight can I lose?

Results vary. NHS sources report figures ranging from about 10–15% body weight in some programmes to average losses up to around 20kg.

Will I feel sick?

Nausea, cramps and vomiting are common in the first days.

Can the balloon burst?

Deflation is uncommon but possible; migration can require urgent assessment.

Will weight come back?

It can, especially if long-term eating/activity habits are not established.
