Bariatric Surgery in Europe: Costs, Safety, Aftercare & UK Comparison

Written and researched by Matt O’Brien, founder of Shift Some Timber.

A UK-focused guide to travelling to Europe for bariatric surgery: current price examples, insurance, GHIC/EHIC limits, aftercare, complications, provider checks and how to compare total cost.

Why do people travel for bariatric surgery?

The obvious reason is price. A sleeve or bypass that costs £10,000–£14,000 privately in the UK may be advertised for roughly half that amount in parts of Europe. Shorter waiting times and packaged travel can also be attractive.

That does not automatically make overseas treatment reckless. It does mean you need to compare the entire care pathway, not just the operation.

Current Europe cost examples

As one current provider example, Nordbariatric in Lithuania advertises sleeve, Roux-en-Y bypass and mini bypass from about €6,150 / £5,450, gastric band around €4,200 / £3,690 and a 12-month gastric balloon around €3,600 / £3,190.

Those are provider-specific examples, not “the European price”. Prices change and packages differ.

Europe is not one healthcare market

Lithuania, Poland, Spain, Latvia, Czechia and other countries all have different providers, regulators, surgical cultures and aftercare models. Judge the hospital and surgeon — not a flag emoji and a Trustpilot score.

GHIC/EHIC does not cover planned surgery abroad

This catches people out.

The NHS says an EHIC or GHIC does not cover travelling abroad specifically for planned medical treatment. It covers necessary state healthcare that becomes medically necessary during a visit.

You therefore need appropriate travel/medical insurance that explicitly covers planned bariatric treatment and complications. Standard holiday insurance often excludes elective surgery.

Flying home after bariatric surgery

Recent surgery, obesity and air travel can all increase venous-thromboembolism risk. Your surgeon should give a personalised plan for when it is safe to fly, walking, hydration and clot prevention.

A package that gets you on a plane as fast as possible is not necessarily doing you a favour.

What if something goes wrong after you get home?

Leaks, bleeding, dehydration, infection, obstruction, bile reflux and blood clots can occur after bariatric surgery. NHS emergency care will treat urgent complications, but it may not have immediate access to your operative notes or surgeon.

Before travelling, obtain the full operative report, implant/device details where relevant, discharge summary, medication list and emergency contact details in English.

Who owns your aftercare?

Ask who orders blood tests, prescribes lifelong supplements, reviews reflux, supports diet progression and manages weight regain. If the answer is “your GP”, make sure your GP has actually agreed to that plan.

NICE stresses ongoing monitoring after discharge from specialist bariatric care. Surgery abroad does not remove that need.

Checks before booking any overseas bariatric provider

  • Surgeon qualifications and specialist registration in that country
  • Hospital accreditation
  • Procedure volume and complication rates
  • English-language consent and operative records
  • Who provides emergency cover
  • How long you remain locally after surgery
  • What happens if you need revision
  • Explicit insurance cover for elective-treatment complications
  • Named long-term dietetic/nutritional support

A separate note on Turkey

Turkey is not in the EU but is a major destination for UK medical tourism, so people often mentally include it in the same conversation. Current FCDO travel advice says it is aware of 7 British nationals who died in Turkey in 2025 following medical procedures, and notes that some British nationals have experienced complications requiring further treatment or surgery.

That does not mean every Turkish bariatric provider is unsafe. It does mean price-led decision-making without serious due diligence is reckless.

When could surgery abroad make sense?

It can make sense when the provider is demonstrably high quality, the surgeon is appropriately trained, you understand the procedure, insurance is adequate, you can stay long enough after surgery, and there is a genuine documented aftercare pathway when you return.

If the only reason the package wins is “£4,000 cheaper and someone picks me up at the airport”, keep looking.

Shift Says

Our take

We are not anti-Europe and we are not automatically pro-UK. Brilliant and poor healthcare exists on both sides of the Channel.

We are anti-headline-price thinking. Compare surgeon, hospital, aftercare, insurance, complication plan and total cost. Then compare price.
