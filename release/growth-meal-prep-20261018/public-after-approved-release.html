Meal prep for busy men: a two-meal plan for a busy week

Food plans, recipes and shopping lists are available in Shift Grub, inside My Timber. Sign in to save your choices.

Meal prep starts with the point in your week when food becomes a nuisance. Pick two meals that usually catch you out, then make those easier. A useful first attempt might be one dinner with an extra portion and one lunch you can put together quickly.

You do not need a new personality, matching containers or a Sunday spent cooking food you will resent by Wednesday. You need a plan you are likely to use.

Choose two real moments

Look at the next few days before deciding what to cook. For example, Tuesday might mean getting home late, while Wednesday lunch usually means buying whatever is nearest. Those are two specific problems you can prepare for.

Write them down: “Tuesday dinner” and “Wednesday lunch”. Check where you will be eating, whether there is a fridge and whether reheating is possible. A brilliant microwave lunch is less brilliant when your workplace has no microwave.

If the week is unpredictable, choose one meal and a cupboard backup instead. Start at a size you can repeat.

Shop from your kitchen first

Open the fridge, freezer and cupboard. Make a short list of ingredients you can already use, then choose the meals. This is easier than buying a new set of ingredients for every recipe.

An illustrative list could include beans, tinned tomatoes, frozen vegetables, potatoes and wholemeal bread. Add ingredients that suit your tastes, allergies and household. This is a planning example rather than a tested recipe or a prescribed diet.

Think about how the meals fit into the wider week. The NHS Eatwell Guide describes balance across the day or week, rather than requiring every plate to match a diagram. Its food groups include vegetables, starchy foods and protein foods such as beans, fish, eggs and meat. Use that as context, not another task to mark yourself against.

Give each meal a different job

For the late dinner, choose something you already enjoy and know how to prepare. If it is suitable for batch cooking and freezing, make an extra portion when you next cook it. Label that portion so it has a clear purpose rather than becoming an unidentified object at the back of the freezer.

  • Cook a meal you already like in a larger batch, if the recipe can be safely scaled, and freeze the extra portions.

For lunch, keep the preparation simpler. You might plan a sandwich with a filling you like, a piece of fruit and something else that fits your appetite and dietary needs. You might prefer soup, or a portion of a suitable cooked meal. Choose what you will want to eat, and plan safe storage and transport.

The two meals do not need to look identical. One can solve a time problem; the other can solve the problem of making a decision while hungry and busy.

Put storage into the plan

Food safety is part of preparation. Follow packet instructions and use-by dates. The Food Standards Agency guidance linked below advises cooling cooked food and refrigerating it within one to two hours, and eating refrigerated leftovers within 48 hours or freezing them if that will not be possible. It advises defrosting in the fridge and using food within 24 hours of complete defrosting. Different foods can have additional precautions; check the guidance relevant to what you are preparing.

Decide where the food will go before cooking. Check there is room, use suitable containers and put the date on them. If a meal depends on remembering to take it to work, leave yourself a reminder where you will see it.

Cooling, storage and reheating

Follow the Food Standards Agency guidance on cooling, chilling and reheating. Cool cooked food promptly, refrigerate it within two hours and reheat leftovers only once until steaming hot throughout. Rice has specific storage precautions; use the linked guidance rather than applying a general rule to every ingredient. If you cannot use a portion within its safe storage time, freeze it promptly if suitable.

Keep one easy alternative

A backup is useful when the plan meets real life. Pick something you can make from ingredients you usually keep in. Write it down beside the two planned meals.

The alternative should be easy enough to use on a difficult evening. If it needs a special shopping trip or a lengthy recipe, it is another plan rather than a backup.

Check what actually helped

After the two meals, ask three questions. Did I eat what I prepared? Did it make the awkward moment easier? Would I choose it again?

If a portion stayed untouched, look at the practical reason. You may have forgotten it, disliked it, packed too much or needed something you could eat cold. Change that detail for the next attempt.

Your next step is small: choose the next awkward meal and write down how you will make it easier. When that works, repeat it. Add more only when more would help.
