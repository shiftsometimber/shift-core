# Calories explained without the nonsense

Calories describe the energy in food and drink. Understanding them can make portions less confusing. You do not need to turn every meal into a maths exam.

## A guide is not your personal target

The NHS gives 2,500 kcal a day as a guide for an average man. It also explains that needs vary with age, weight, height and activity. That number is not a universal weight-loss target, and a calculator gives an estimate rather than an exact daily requirement.

Use the information to understand your usual meals. Individual dietary needs, medical conditions and treatment belong in a conversation with an appropriate qualified professional.

## Read the portion as well as the number

On a food label, look for **energy**, usually shown in kcal and kJ. Nutrition information is given per 100g or 100ml, and sometimes per portion. The manufacturer's portion may be different from yours.

Here is a made-up label to show the calculation. It is not a recommended meal size:

| What the label or packet says | Energy |
| --- | --- |
| Per 100g | 160 kcal |
| Whole packet: 350g | 560 kcal |
| Half the packet: 175g | 280 kcal |

The whole-packet calculation is 160 × 3.5. If you eat half, that is half the packet's energy. Add anything you serve with it if you are keeping a record.

The point is to understand what the number covers. A packet labelled as two servings does not become one serving because you opened it alone.

## Compare similar foods fairly

The per-100g figure gives you a common starting point when comparing similar products. Then check the amount you expect to eat. Comparing one manufacturer's small serving with another's larger serving can be misleading.

Look beyond calories too. Labels also give information about fat, saturated fat, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. A lower energy number alone does not describe the whole food or tell you whether that meal suits your needs.

## Think across the day or week

The NHS Eatwell Guide describes the balance of food groups across your overall diet. You do not have to reproduce its proportions at every meal: aim for balance across a day or even a week.

For a practical planning example, look at tomorrow's lunch and dinner together. What are you already having? Where could vegetables, fruit or another food group fit into the wider day? Choose something you enjoy and can realistically prepare. These are planning questions, not a prescribed menu or a reason to add oil to a meal just to make it look balanced.

## Try one useful check

Choose one packaged food you buy regularly. Read the per-100g figure, the packet weight and any stated serving size. Work out what the portion you usually eat contains. Write down one thing you learnt.

If you are tracking, remember the things that can slip out of the record: drinks, dressings, cooking oil and extras. If you are not tracking, you can still use a label to understand a portion or compare two similar products.

## Keep the tool in its place

Tracking is optional. If counting makes you anxious, restrictive or preoccupied with food, seek suitable professional advice rather than pushing harder at the numbers. Do not compensate for eating by skipping needed food or forcing exercise. If you have an eating-disorder history, discuss weight-loss tracking with an appropriate professional first.

After trying an approach, ask: could I manage it on a busy day, was I adequately fed, and did it make an ordinary meal easier? If it creates more work than it solves, simplify it or ask for support.

Your next step can be one label and one useful discovery. You can also [explore the calorie estimate tool](https://shiftsometimber.co.uk/tools/calories), remembering that its result is an estimate.
